It’s a langorously warm autumn in New York City, and a year since my last dispatch, as I’ve erred on the side of silence while reaching for a natural publishing cadence. Today I’m thrilled to release Unda Maris, an audio electronics device I’ve developed over the last year. It concludes an informal trilogy of expanded audio works concerning environment and the act of listening; previous works include Frog Chorus and Airs. Unda Maris is a work of generative electronic music in the form of a touch-responsive audio device, and it traces themes of psychoacoustics, composing listening, interface design, tuning systems, and the oceanic. You can read more about it on the Metalabel platform [here](link tk), where I have copies for sale from the edition of 25. I’ll also have some events around it in NYC over the coming months, starting with a listening launch at Ergot Records on ((December TK)). I hope to see some of you there!

I’ll also continue to sell ceramic home goods on my web shop, where I have a new section for incense trays. These flared and floating forms are perhaps reminiscent of both Lucy Rie’s bowls and the conical design of audio speakers (see also the Japanese tradition of monkō: “listening to incense”). I’ve also been experimenting with a variation on the same form, adding a snip of nichrome wire that works as a post for candles.

Separately from the studio process of developing these objects, I’ve been struggling with an ambivalence about how to present them as product images. I recently read Janet Malcolm’s final collection of memoir-essays, Still Photos, wherein she describes her early writing career at the New Yorker writing a shopping guides for a high-bourgeois audience, but without the aide of product images.

In this generative image, the indexical function of the product image is displaced into the latent space navigated by language itself, where Malcolm was writing from in the 1960s https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1966/09/10/about-the-house. On Shopify’s evergreen page on developing MVPs, they emphasize the early deployment of marketing materials as a means of validating the product’s market potential before investment in production, a strategy enabled by the prevalance of dropshipping. The product image has moved from proof of life to proof of sale, and in so becoming an image-product. These image-products also exist as free-floating speculative objects in the emergent trend of dopamine shops, “fake” shopping sites that exist only to stoke and satsify desire by simulating the shopping and checkout experience.

“Thus, in adult life, the quest is not to possess the object; rather the object is pursued in order to surrender to it as a medium that alters the self” Bollas

The transformational promise of the image-product is perhaps a short-circuiting of the human valences of object and desire described by the British psychoanalyst Christopher Bollas in “The Shadow of the Object.” I’ll say also that I buy little these days in the way of non-essential lifestyle goods, that my practice of object-making seems to have diverted much of my drive to possess objects. The object never really exists, and has never really existed, but in its shadow. As Bollas writes, “An object is first a process.”
